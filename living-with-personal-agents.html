Living with Agents

Article · 2026 Q4

A while ago, I wrote a short article about OS for agents. I talked about the mental model for managing agents and the mixed initiative interaction problem. What I didn’t realize was that there will be so many personal agents coming to the market in the later half of the year. After using some of the more popular agents for a while, I have some new perspectives on this subject matter.

One entity or a team?

In my previous article I laid out the human-agent relationship on a spectrum based on the number of agents we will interact with, from one agent as the executive assistant, to a small council as your life coaches/mentors, to a marketplace of agents as your task based contractors.The current landscape of agents (as of late 2026) simplifies my spectrum into two polar:

One agent who does it all for you. Usually for your personal life.

  • Pros: The entry point to the interaction is the simplest. You don’t need to think about which agent to talk to. This sounds small on paper but if you put it into the situation of interacting with the agent dozens of times per day, maybe most of them are on-the-go, it becomes a huge deal. If companies plan to put their agent on their wearables, the one agent mental model is almost a must.
  • Cons: All tasks and conversations are blended together in one chat window. The one-off asks are mixed with long back-and-forth deep discussion on a topic. If there is a particular response from the past you want to reference or examine again, it can be hard to dig it up from all the noises.

Multiple agents who can work together to form a workflow, or separately to cover different aspects of work/life. Usually for your work, business or projects.

  • Pros: Dedicated roles and responsibilities for each agent means your context and tasks are well categorized. You can also get second opinions if you ask for advice in a group chat.
  • Cons: this multi-agent model works great in a well defined work/project environment, but the ever-changing personal life cannot be as well defined. We need to handle miscellaneous stuff that is difficult to put a clear label on. Fencing our lives into separate territories and assigning an agent on each is just not very realistic. Try to do so and you will soon find yourself debating which agent to talk to, or creating more and more agents for the longtail cases, or both.

A mirror or a hand?

At a very high level, we want two things from the AI agent:

  • We wanted it to be a mirror, reflecting our context and data back to us in the form of insights and coaching advice, helping us understand ourselves deeper and overtime become a better version of ourselves.
  • We want it to be an extra hand, doing the tasks we were not capable of or too busy to do before.

One AI agent can switch its role between mirror and hand for us from task to task, but it cannot be both in the same session. The hand listens to orders and prioritizes getting things done. The mirror questions what you throw at it and worries about you doing the wrong thing. Given a specific request, the two roles conflict with each other. The agent has to choose one to play when responding back. Knowing which to pick based on the state of the conversation and available context is critical. It sets the tongue and the direction of how it should respond to us.

Alignment (at personal level, not at humanity scale)

Trust is a must and alignment is one of the cornerstones to build trust. Alignment should happen across altitude at 3 levels: memories, goals, tasks.

  • Memories: high altitude, an understanding of the user based on accumulated context
  • Goals: mid altitude, description of a future state, what the user wants to achieve
  • Tasks: low altitude, a future action that the agent will take to help achieve a goal, more granular, specific

A full alignment requires users to be able to view and edit all 3 altitudes.

Because agents have longer term memories and can proactively follow tasks, a full alignment requires users to be able to view and edit all 3 altitudes so that users can have confidence the agents are working towards the right trajectory in the background.

My space, their space or our space?

At low altitude, where agents are doing the task on a computer, they could be doing it in 3 different kinds of space:

  • Mine: I gave the agent access to my space but oftentimes I would like to keep an eye on what it does there and expect the agent to only touch and change things that I explicitly allow it to. Examples could be my calendar, my messages, my health data.
  • Theirs: The agents’ own VM. They have the most freedom to do things their way there. I might take a peak if I’m curious. But most of the time I leave them alone.
  • Ours: Me and my agents’ shared space. The agent can go and make changes at any time. And I can do the same too. But most importantly, this is the space we can edit together at the same time. Version history is probably a must.

Connect the ownership of space with the quadrant diagram for attention and intent from my earlier article, and we will get this table:

WorkspaceWork StyleHuman Involvement
Agents working in my spaceSuperviseHigh attention and low intent density
Agents working in their spaceDelegate, Hand off,Low attention and low intent density; Low attention and high intent density
Agents and I working together in a shared spaceCollaborateHigh attention and high intent density

Tangled threads

All agents use the interface model of the messaging application. This model assumes equal importance on all messages, which means all the interactions are organized chronologically as chat bubbles. This creates two problems:

  • One thread tangles everything together.
  • Long topics/tasks could be scattered across the timeline.

If I forgot about something mentioned earlier, or want to reference a previous response, I will have to scroll through the long messy chat history. The experience is much worse than doing it in a human-to-human chat because agents tend to respond with long blocks of text. Sometimes I just ask the agent the same question again instead of searching in the history myself.

But if the intention is not to look back at the chat history, then why organize it like this in the first place?

It seems like a better way would be to organize past conversations into topics with widgets like summaries, progress trackers or action cards, etc.

Blank canvas

“I’m not travelling and I don’t have subscriptions to cancel. What can an AI agent do for me?” When facing the empty text input box, people need inspiration to help them understand what kind of work they can hand over to the agent.

Currently, apps like Muse have a dedicated ideas tab to show users the breadth of work it can take on. Beyond that, discovery of agent use cases heavily relies on word of mouth, yet everyone’s life is different. In order for the agent to show what it can do for me specifically, not the hypothetical person in the commercial, it needs my context; but I don’t feel confident to give it access to my context without knowing what it will do on behalf of me. This is the dilemma of a deeply personalized, context dependent product like an AI agent.

What makes the situation worse, is that the types of work agents can do keeps expanding as the model and the architecture improves in the background, while the way we interface with it stays the same. So users won’t have any ideas of what becomes possible now that was not possible before by just looking at the interface.

These 2 problems combined, the dilemma of use case discovery, and the opacity of the ever changing capability boundary, makes the blank canvas on the agent interface harder to overcome.
